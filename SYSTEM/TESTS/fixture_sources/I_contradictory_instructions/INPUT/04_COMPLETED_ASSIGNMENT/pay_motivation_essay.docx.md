# Critically discuss whether performance-related pay improves employee motivation
HRM5003 Human Resource Management in Practice – Assessment 1 – Candidate 0000 – Northbridge University
## Introduction
Performance-related pay (PRP) links part of an employee's earnings to an assessment of their individual, team or organisational performance. It takes many forms, including merit pay increases, individual bonuses, commission and profit-sharing schemes. PRP has become widespread in both the private and public sectors because it appears to offer a simple solution to two management problems: how to motivate employees to work harder and how to reward high performers fairly. However, whether PRP actually improves motivation is contested. This essay critically discusses the question by applying two theoretical perspectives, expectancy theory and self-determination theory, and by evaluating the empirical evidence. It argues that PRP can improve motivation for simple, measurable tasks when schemes are well designed and perceived as fair, but that it can weaken intrinsic motivation for complex and interesting work. The effect of PRP therefore depends on the type of work, the design of the scheme and the way it is managed.
## Expectancy theory and performance-related pay
Expectancy theory offers the strongest theoretical case for PRP. Vroom (1964) proposed that motivation is the product of three beliefs: expectancy (the belief that effort will lead to performance), instrumentality (the belief that performance will lead to a reward) and valence (the value the individual places on that reward). If any of these beliefs is weak, motivation will be low. PRP is designed to strengthen instrumentality by making the link between performance and pay explicit. An employee who knows that meeting a sales target will result in a bonus has a clear reason to put in extra effort, provided that the bonus is valued and the target is achievable.
There is empirical evidence that supports this reasoning. Lazear (2000) studied a large American car-glass installation company that replaced hourly wages with piece-rate pay. Productivity rose by around 44 per cent, partly because existing employees worked harder and partly because the scheme attracted more productive workers and discouraged less productive ones. This suggests that PRP can influence both motivation and the composition of the workforce. Gerhart and Fang (2015) review a wide body of research and conclude that pay incentives generally have a positive effect on performance, particularly where performance can be measured objectively.
However, expectancy theory also highlights the conditions under which PRP fails. Expectancy will be low if employees believe that targets are unrealistic or that their performance depends on factors outside their control, such as the performance of colleagues or the state of the market. Instrumentality will be weak if employees do not trust the appraisal process, for example if ratings are seen as subjective or influenced by favouritism. In many organisations the amount of pay at risk is also small, so the valence of the reward may be too low to change behaviour. From an expectancy perspective, then, PRP does not automatically improve motivation; it improves motivation only when employees believe that effort leads to performance, that performance is accurately measured and rewarded, and that the reward is worth having. These conditions are difficult to achieve in roles where performance is ambiguous, such as in teaching, healthcare or many knowledge-based jobs. In such roles, managers often rely on subjective appraisal ratings, which weakens instrumentality and can lead employees to see the scheme as arbitrary. Expectancy theory therefore explains both why PRP can work and why it frequently disappoints in practice.
## Self-determination theory and performance-related pay
Self-determination theory (SDT) provides a more critical perspective. Deci and Ryan (1985) distinguish between intrinsic motivation, where a person performs an activity because it is interesting and satisfying in itself, and extrinsic motivation, where the activity is performed to obtain a separate outcome such as money. SDT proposes that people have three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. When these needs are satisfied, people are more likely to be intrinsically motivated and to perform well over the long term.
According to SDT, the effect of a reward depends on how it is experienced. If a reward is perceived as controlling, it reduces a person's sense of autonomy and can undermine intrinsic motivation, which is often described as the crowding-out effect. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) conducted a meta-analysis of 128 experiments and found that tangible rewards that were expected and contingent on task performance significantly reduced intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks. This finding is a direct challenge to the assumption behind PRP. If employees begin to focus on the bonus rather than on the work itself, they may lose interest in the task, reduce effort once the reward is removed, and concentrate only on the activities that are measured.
However, SDT does not claim that all rewards are harmful. Rewards that are perceived as informational, in other words as recognition of competence, can support rather than undermine motivation. Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford (2014) found in a meta-analysis that intrinsic motivation was a stronger predictor of the quality of performance, while extrinsic incentives were a stronger predictor of the quantity of performance. Importantly, they found that intrinsic motivation and incentives can operate together, and that the negative effect of incentives on intrinsic motivation was strongest when incentives were closely tied to performance. This suggests that PRP may be most suitable for routine, output-based work and least suitable for complex, creative or professional work.
## Critical evaluation
Comparing the two perspectives shows that the question of whether PRP improves motivation cannot be answered simply. Expectancy theory and the evidence from Lazear (2000) show that incentives can raise effort and output when performance is easy to measure. SDT and the evidence from Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) show that incentives can damage intrinsic motivation, particularly for interesting tasks. Much of the SDT evidence comes from laboratory experiments with students, which may not reflect workplace conditions where pay is expected and necessary. Gerhart and Fang (2015) argue that the crowding-out effect has sometimes been overstated in management debates, and that the sorting effect of pay, attracting and retaining high performers, is also important.
A further issue is that PRP can have unintended consequences for behaviour. When targets are narrow, employees may neglect unmeasured aspects of their jobs, such as helping colleagues or maintaining quality. Individual PRP may also damage teamwork and create perceptions of unfairness, which would reduce the relatedness need identified in SDT. For HR managers, this suggests that the design of PRP matters as much as its existence. Schemes that use achievable targets, transparent measures, team as well as individual elements, and recognition that supports employees' sense of competence are more likely to be motivating.
## Conclusion
This essay has critically discussed whether performance-related pay improves employee motivation. Expectancy theory suggests that PRP can increase motivation by strengthening the link between performance and reward, and there is evidence that incentives increase effort and productivity where output can be measured. However, self-determination theory warns that contingent rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation, especially for interesting and complex work, and can encourage a narrow focus on measured targets. The essay concludes that PRP does not improve motivation in all circumstances. It is most likely to be effective for routine and measurable work, when schemes are perceived as fair and achievable, and when rewards are presented as recognition of competence rather than as a means of control. For complex professional roles, organisations should combine modest performance rewards with job design that supports autonomy, competence and relatedness.
