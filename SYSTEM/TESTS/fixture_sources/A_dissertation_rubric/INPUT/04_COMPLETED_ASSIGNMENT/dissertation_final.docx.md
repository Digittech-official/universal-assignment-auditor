# The Effect of Flexible Working Arrangements on Employee Retention in UK Small and Medium-sized Enterprises
MSc Business Management – BUS7021 Mini-Dissertation – Candidate 0000 – Northbridge University
## Abstract
This mini-dissertation examines whether flexible working arrangements (FWAs) help UK small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to retain employees. An online questionnaire was completed by 48 employees working in 11 SMEs in the North of England. Descriptive statistics showed that flexitime and hybrid working were the most common arrangements, while compressed hours were rare. Employees with access to two or more arrangements reported higher job satisfaction and stronger intentions to stay than employees with no access. The results suggest that flexibility is a low-cost retention tool for SMEs, although its effect depends on how consistently it is applied by line managers. The study recommends that SME managers formalise a simple flexible working policy, train supervisors to handle requests fairly and review retention data annually.
## 1. Introduction
Employee turnover is costly for all organisations, but it is particularly damaging for SMEs, which have fewer staff to absorb the loss of skills and knowledge when an employee leaves. SMEs account for over 99% of UK businesses and around three fifths of private sector employment, yet they often cannot compete with large firms on salary or promotion opportunities. Flexible working has therefore been promoted as a way for smaller employers to make themselves attractive without large increases in pay costs.
Most academic research on flexible working has been carried out in large organisations, and much less is known about how it operates in SMEs, where policies are often informal and depend on the owner or line manager. This study addresses that gap.
### 1.1 Aim
The aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of flexible working arrangements on employee retention in UK SMEs.
### 1.2 Objectives
Objective 1: To identify the types of flexible working arrangements available to employees in UK SMEs.
Objective 2: To assess the effect of flexible working arrangements on employees' job satisfaction.
Objective 3: To examine the relationship between access to flexible working arrangements and employees' intention to stay with their employer.
### 1.3 Research Questions
RQ1: Which flexible working arrangements are available to employees in UK SMEs?
RQ2: How do flexible working arrangements affect the job satisfaction of SME employees?
RQ3: Is access to flexible working arrangements associated with a stronger intention to stay?
## 2. Literature Review
### 2.1 Defining flexible working
Flexible working refers to arrangements that give employees some choice over when, where or how much they work. Common forms include flexitime, remote or hybrid working, part-time hours, job sharing and compressed working weeks. Allen et al. (2013) argue that flexibility should not be treated as a single concept, because flexibility in time (flexitime) and flexibility in place (remote working) have different consequences for employees.
### 2.2 Flexible working and employee attitudes
A large body of research links flexible working to positive employee attitudes. Gajendran and Harrison (2007) found that telecommuting was associated with higher job satisfaction and lower turnover intention, mainly because it increased employees' perceived autonomy. Similarly, Kossek et al. (2014) show that employees who feel they have control over their working patterns report lower stress and greater commitment. However, the relationship is not always straightforward. Kelliher and Anderson (2010) found that employees who worked flexibly often experienced work intensification, working harder in exchange for the flexibility they had been given. They explain this through social exchange theory: employees feel obliged to repay the organisation for a benefit they value. This suggests that flexible working can increase satisfaction and commitment at the same time as increasing workload.
Chen and Fulmer (2018) add that it is not only the use of flexible working but also its availability that matters. In their study, employees who knew that flexibility was available to them reported more positive attitudes even if they did not use it. This is relevant to SMEs, where arrangements may be available informally but are not written into policy.
### 2.3 Flexible working and retention
Turnover research has traditionally focused on job satisfaction and organisational commitment as the main predictors of employees' decisions to leave (Hom et al., 2017). Job embeddedness theory offers a broader explanation, suggesting that employees stay when they have strong links to their organisation and community, a good fit with their job and when leaving would involve significant sacrifices (Mitchell et al., 2001). Flexible working may increase embeddedness because it would be a sacrifice to give up a flexible arrangement by moving to a less flexible employer.
### 2.4 The SME context
Most of the studies discussed above were conducted in large organisations with formal human resource departments. In SMEs, decisions about flexible working are often made informally by owners or line managers, which can lead to inconsistent treatment. This means that the benefits identified in the literature may not transfer directly to SMEs. The limited research in this setting justifies the present study.
## 3. Methodology
### 3.1 Research philosophy and approach
The study adopted a positivist philosophy and a deductive approach, as it tested expectations derived from the literature about the relationship between flexible working and retention (Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill, 2019). A quantitative, cross-sectional survey design was chosen because it allowed data to be collected from a number of SMEs within the short timescale of the module.
### 3.2 Sampling
A non-probability convenience sample was used. The researcher contacted 20 SMEs through a regional business network in the North of England, and 11 agreed to circulate the questionnaire to their staff. A total of 48 usable responses were received. Organisations had between 12 and 180 employees, which falls within the UK definition of an SME (fewer than 250 employees).
### 3.3 Data collection
Data were collected using an online questionnaire hosted on Microsoft Forms over a three-week period in March 2026. The questionnaire contained three sections: (1) which flexible working arrangements were available to the respondent; (2) five job satisfaction items; and (3) four intention to stay items adapted from established turnover intention scales. Sections 2 and 3 used five-point Likert scales from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The questionnaire was piloted with three colleagues and two items were reworded for clarity. The full questionnaire is included in Appendix A.
### 3.4 Data analysis
Responses were exported to Microsoft Excel. Mean scores were calculated for the job satisfaction and intention to stay scales. Respondents were grouped by the number of flexible working arrangements available to them (none, one, or two or more), and mean scores were compared across the three groups. Because of the small sample size, the analysis was descriptive and no inferential statistical tests were carried out.
### 3.5 Limitations
The use of a small convenience sample means that the results cannot be generalised to all UK SMEs. Intention to stay was used as a proxy for actual retention, and some respondents may have given socially desirable answers. The cross-sectional design also means that causal relationships cannot be established.
## 4. Findings
### 4.1 Availability of flexible working arrangements
Of the 48 respondents, 39 (81%) had access to at least one flexible working arrangement. Flexitime was the most commonly available arrangement (27 respondents, 56%), followed by hybrid or remote working (22 respondents, 46%) and part-time hours (15 respondents, 31%). Only 4 respondents (8%) had access to a compressed working week, and none reported job sharing. Respondents in professional services were most likely to have access to hybrid working, while those in hospitality and retail mainly had access to part-time hours.
### 4.2 Job satisfaction and intention to stay
Table 1 compares mean job satisfaction and intention to stay scores by the number of arrangements available to the respondent.
| Access to flexible working | Number of respondents | Mean job satisfaction (1–5) | Mean intention to stay (1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| No arrangements | 9 | 2.9 | 2.7 |
| One arrangement | 17 | 3.5 | 3.4 |
| Two or more arrangements | 22 | 4.0 | 4.1 |
| All respondents | 48 | 3.6 | 3.6 |
Table 1: Mean job satisfaction and intention to stay scores by access to flexible working (n = 48).
Both job satisfaction and intention to stay increased with the number of flexible working arrangements available. Employees with two or more arrangements had a mean intention to stay of 4.1, compared with 2.7 for employees with none. Of the 22 respondents with two or more arrangements, 18 agreed or strongly agreed that they planned to be working for the same employer in two years' time, compared with only 2 of the 9 respondents with no arrangements. In an optional comment box, several respondents said that flexibility was the main reason they had not looked for another job, while three respondents said that their manager approved requests inconsistently.
## 5. Discussion
The findings support the literature suggesting that flexible working is associated with positive employee attitudes. The higher job satisfaction scores among employees with more arrangements are consistent with Gajendran and Harrison (2007), who linked flexibility to autonomy and satisfaction. The pattern for intention to stay is also consistent with job embeddedness theory (Mitchell et al., 2001): employees who enjoy several forms of flexibility would have more to lose by moving to a less flexible employer.
The results also support Chen and Fulmer (2018), as the availability of arrangements appeared to matter. Several respondents in the "two or more" group reported that they rarely used hybrid working but valued knowing that it was available. This is important for SMEs, because it suggests that offering flexibility need not be expensive or disruptive if employees use it selectively.
However, the comments about inconsistent approval highlight a risk specific to SMEs. Where flexible working depends on individual managers rather than a written policy, employees may perceive unfairness, which could reduce the retention benefit. This extends the argument of Kelliher and Anderson (2010) that the way flexibility is implemented shapes its outcomes. The finding that hospitality and retail staff mainly had access to part-time hours rather than flexitime or hybrid working also supports Allen et al. (2013), who argue that different forms of flexibility are not equivalent.
Finally, the results should be interpreted with caution. The differences in Table 1 are descriptive and based on small groups, and it is possible that more satisfied employees work for better-managed firms that also happen to offer more flexibility.
## 6. Conclusion and Recommendations
### 6.1 Conclusion
This study set out to evaluate the effect of flexible working arrangements on employee retention in UK SMEs. In relation to Objective 1, the study found that most SME employees had access to at least one arrangement, with flexitime and hybrid working being the most common, although access varied considerably by sector. In relation to Objective 3, the study found a clear positive relationship between the number of arrangements available and employees' intention to stay, with employees who had two or more arrangements reporting much stronger intentions to remain with their employer than those with none. Overall, the study concludes that flexible working is a valuable and relatively low-cost retention tool for SMEs, but that its benefits depend on it being applied fairly and consistently.
### 6.2 Recommendations
First, SME managers should introduce a short written flexible working policy that sets out which arrangements are available, how employees can request them and how quickly requests will be answered, which would also support compliance with the statutory right to request flexible working.
Second, owners should give line managers brief training on handling flexible working requests, focusing on fair and consistent decision-making and on how to plan workloads so that flexibility does not lead to work intensification.
Third, SMEs in sectors such as retail and hospitality, where hybrid working is not practical, should consider shift-swapping systems or self-rostering to offer some control over working time.
Finally, SME managers should monitor voluntary turnover and exit interview data each year to assess whether flexible working is helping to retain staff. Future research should use a larger sample and track actual turnover over time.
