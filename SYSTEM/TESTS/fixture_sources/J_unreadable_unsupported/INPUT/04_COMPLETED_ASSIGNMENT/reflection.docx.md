# Reflection on communicating with an anxious patient before a procedure
Candidate 0000 – NUR2001 Professional Practice 2
## Description
During my second-year placement on a surgical ward, I was asked by my practice supervisor to help prepare an older patient, whom I will call Mr A to protect confidentiality, for a planned procedure later that morning. When I arrived to complete his pre-procedure observations, Mr A was restless and repeatedly asked whether the procedure would be painful and whether he would "wake up afterwards". I began to explain the checklist I needed to complete, but he became more agitated and said that nobody had told him what was happening. I stopped the checklist, sat down beside him and asked what he already understood. I then fetched my practice supervisor, who explained the procedure and arranged for the anaesthetist to speak to him. The procedure went ahead as planned.
## Feelings
At first I felt anxious and under pressure, because I was aware that the patient needed to be ready on time and I did not want to delay the list. When Mr A became agitated, I felt uncomfortable and worried that I had made the situation worse by focusing on the checklist rather than on him. Once I sat down and listened, I felt calmer and more in control. Afterwards I felt relieved that he had received the information he needed, but I was also disappointed in myself for not noticing his anxiety sooner.
## Evaluation
Some aspects of the situation went well. I recognised that Mr A was distressed, stopped what I was doing and gave him my full attention. I also knew the limits of my own knowledge and escalated to my practice supervisor rather than trying to answer clinical questions I was not qualified to answer. However, my initial approach was task-focused. I started with the checklist because I was concerned about time, and this made Mr A feel that his concerns were less important than the paperwork. With hindsight, I should have introduced myself properly and asked how he was feeling before starting any tasks.
## Analysis
On reflection, my initial behaviour reflected the pressure I felt to complete tasks efficiently, which is common among student nurses who want to be seen as competent. However, the NMC Code requires nurses to treat people as individuals, to listen to them and respond to their preferences and concerns, and to make sure they have the information they need (Nursing and Midwifery Council, 2018). By prioritising the checklist, I did not fully meet this standard at the start of the interaction. Pre-procedure anxiety is common, and patients who do not understand what is going to happen are more likely to feel frightened and out of control. Taking time to listen is therefore not a delay to care but part of it.
Bulman and Schutz (2013) argue that reflection helps practitioners to recognise the assumptions behind their actions. Reflecting on this event, I recognise that I assumed Mr A had already been given a full explanation because he had signed a consent form. In fact, consent and understanding are not the same thing, and anxiety can prevent patients from retaining information. Using Gibbs' (1988) cycle has helped me to move beyond simply describing what happened to understanding why I acted as I did and what the consequences were for the patient.
I also recognise that escalating to my practice supervisor was appropriate. It ensured that Mr A received accurate information from staff qualified to provide it and was consistent with the NMC requirement to work within the limits of one's competence (Nursing and Midwifery Council, 2018).
## Conclusion
This experience taught me that effective communication must come before routine tasks, especially when patients are anxious. I learned that a signed consent form does not guarantee that a patient feels informed, and that a few minutes spent listening can prevent distress and improve the patient's experience. I also learned that it is appropriate and professional to ask for help when a patient's questions go beyond my knowledge. If a similar situation happened again, I would introduce myself, ask the patient how they were feeling and check their understanding before beginning any checklist.
## Action plan
First, at the start of every patient interaction I will introduce myself and ask how the patient is feeling before beginning any tasks. Second, I will ask my practice supervisor for feedback on my communication with anxious patients during the remainder of this placement. Third, I will complete the university's online module on communication skills in clinical settings before my next placement. Finally, I will continue to use Gibbs' (1988) reflective cycle after significant events so that I can keep learning from my practice.
