# Urban Heat Islands and Mitigation Strategies for a Mid-Sized City
ENV2004 Urban Environments – Individual Report – Candidate 0000
## 1. Introduction
An urban heat island (UHI) is an urban area that is significantly warmer than its surrounding rural areas. The difference is usually greatest at night and on calm, clear days, when urban air temperatures can be several degrees higher than those in the nearby countryside (Oke, 1982). Urban heat islands matter because higher temperatures increase energy demand for cooling, worsen air quality and raise the risk of heat-related illness, particularly for older people and those with existing health conditions. As summers become hotter, these impacts are expected to grow. This report explains the main causes of urban heat islands and evaluates three mitigation strategies that could be adopted by a mid-sized city of around 200,000 people: cool roofs, urban greening and reflective pavements. It concludes with recommendations on how these strategies could be combined.
## 2. Causes of urban heat islands
The urban heat island effect results from several interacting causes that alter the surface energy balance of cities (Oke, 1982).
### 2.1 Surface materials
Many urban materials, such as asphalt, concrete and dark roofing, have a low albedo, which means they absorb a large proportion of incoming solar radiation rather than reflecting it. They also have a high thermal capacity, so they store heat during the day and release it slowly at night. This is one of the main reasons why urban heat islands are often strongest after sunset (Santamouris, 2014).
### 2.2 Loss of vegetation
Vegetation and moist soils cool their surroundings through evapotranspiration, in which energy is used to evaporate water rather than to heat the air. When green spaces are replaced by buildings and paved surfaces, this cooling effect is lost and more of the available energy goes into warming the surface and the air above it (Bowler et al., 2010).
### 2.3 Urban geometry
Tall buildings and narrow streets form "urban canyons" that trap heat. Radiation reflected from one surface is often absorbed by another, and the restricted view of the sky reduces the amount of long-wave radiation that can escape at night. Buildings also reduce wind speeds, limiting the removal of warm air (Oke, 1982).
### 2.4 Anthropogenic heat
Human activities release heat directly into the urban environment. Vehicles, industry, and the heating and cooling of buildings all contribute. In hot weather, the use of air conditioning can create a feedback loop, since the waste heat released by cooling systems further warms the outside air (Akbari, Pomerantz and Taha, 2001).
## 3. Mitigation strategies
### 3.1 Cool roofs
Cool roofs use highly reflective and emissive materials, such as white membranes or specialist coatings, to reflect more sunlight and release absorbed heat more quickly. Santamouris (2014) reviewed a large number of studies and found that increasing the albedo of roofs across a city can reduce average ambient temperatures by a small but meaningful amount, with larger reductions in peak surface temperatures. Cool roofs also lower indoor temperatures in buildings without air conditioning and reduce cooling energy demand in those that have it (Akbari, Pomerantz and Taha, 2001).
Evaluation: cool roofs are relatively cheap, especially when applied during routine roof replacement, and can be introduced quickly. However, their effect on street-level temperatures is limited because roofs are some distance above pedestrians. In a temperate climate, they may slightly increase heating demand in winter, and their reflectivity declines over time as surfaces become dirty, so maintenance is required. Their effectiveness also depends on the proportion of roofs that are suitable, which may be low in older districts with pitched tiled roofs.
### 3.2 Urban greening
Urban greening includes street trees, parks, green roofs and green walls. Vegetation cools the environment through shading and evapotranspiration. In a systematic review, Bowler et al. (2010) found that urban parks were on average around 1°C cooler than surrounding built-up areas during the day, and that larger parks had a greater cooling effect. A local pilot in Kestrel found that tree planting along two main streets reduced surface temperatures on shaded pavements during summer afternoons (Kestrel City Council, 2021).
Evaluation: urban greening provides many additional benefits, including improved biodiversity, better management of surface water, and positive effects on mental and physical wellbeing. Street trees are particularly effective at improving pedestrian comfort because they provide direct shade. However, trees take many years to reach maturity, require space that may be limited in dense areas, and need ongoing maintenance and watering, especially during droughts when cooling is most needed. The cooling effect of a park also declines with distance from its edge, so a network of smaller green spaces may be more effective than a single large park (Bowler et al., 2010).
### 3.3 Reflective pavements
Reflective or "cool" pavements use lighter-coloured materials, coatings or aggregates to increase the albedo of roads, car parks and pavements. Qin (2015) reviewed a range of cool pavement technologies and reported that they can substantially reduce pavement surface temperatures during hot weather. Because roads and car parks can cover a large share of a city's surface, even modest changes to their reflectivity may influence local temperatures.
Evaluation: reflective pavements can be introduced as part of normal resurfacing programmes, which limits additional costs. However, the reflected radiation can increase the heat load on pedestrians and nearby building walls, which may reduce thermal comfort even while surface temperatures fall (Qin, 2015). Light-coloured surfaces can also cause glare and may darken over time because of traffic and dirt, reducing their effectiveness. For these reasons, reflective pavements may be most suitable for car parks and low-traffic areas rather than busy pedestrian streets.
## 4. Conclusion
Urban heat islands are caused by a combination of heat-absorbing surface materials, the loss of vegetation, urban geometry and anthropogenic heat. Each of the three strategies evaluated in this report addresses some of these causes, but none is sufficient on its own. Cool roofs are cheap and quick to introduce but have limited effects at street level. Urban greening offers the widest range of benefits but takes time and requires space and maintenance. Reflective pavements can cover large areas but may reduce pedestrian comfort if poorly located. For a mid-sized city, the most effective approach would be a combined strategy: requiring cool roofs on new and refurbished flat-roofed buildings, prioritising street trees and small green spaces in the hottest and most vulnerable neighbourhoods, and using reflective surfaces mainly in car parks and low-traffic areas. Monitoring temperatures before and after implementation would allow the city to assess which measures work best in its own conditions.
