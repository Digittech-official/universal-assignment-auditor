# The role of artificial intelligence in personalised learning in higher education

## Introduction

Universities are under growing pressure to support larger and more diverse groups of students while maintaining the quality of teaching. One response to this challenge has been the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to personalise learning, meaning that the content, pace or support a student receives is adapted to their individual needs. Interest in this area has increased quickly in recent years, and a wide range of AI tools are now available to universities. This essay describes the main AI techniques used for personalisation in higher education, outlines the benefits they are said to offer, and discusses some of the risks and ethical concerns that have been raised. It concludes that AI has an important role to play in personalised learning, but that this role should be introduced carefully.

## AI techniques for personalisation

Several different kinds of AI are used to personalise learning. The first is adaptive learning systems. These systems present students with material and questions, record how they perform, and then adjust what comes next. For example, a student who answers a set of statistics questions correctly may move on to a more advanced topic, while a student who struggles may be given further explanations and practice. Zawacki-Richter et al. (2019) found that adaptive systems and personalisation were one of the main areas of research on AI in higher education.

The second technique is intelligent tutoring systems. These aim to imitate some of the behaviour of a human tutor by giving hints, feedback and step-by-step guidance. Luckin et al. (2016) explain that intelligent tutoring systems usually contain a model of the subject being taught, a model of the learner and a model of effective teaching, and that these work together to decide how the system should respond to the student.

The third technique is learning analytics. Universities collect large amounts of data about students, such as their use of the virtual learning environment, attendance and assessment results. AI can be used to analyse this data to identify patterns, for example to predict which students are at risk of failing or dropping out. Staff can then contact these students and offer support. More recently, chatbots and generative AI tools have also been used to answer student questions at any time of day and to provide explanations tailored to the student's level (Holmes, Bialik and Fadel, 2019).

## Benefits

The main benefit of AI for personalised learning is that it allows each student to learn at their own pace. In a large lecture, the teacher usually has to aim at the middle of the group, which means that some students find the material too easy and others find it too difficult. Adaptive systems can reduce this problem by giving each student material at the right level (Holmes, Bialik and Fadel, 2019).

A second benefit is immediate feedback. Students often have to wait several weeks to receive feedback on their work, by which time they may have moved on to another topic. AI tools can provide feedback straight away, which helps students to correct mistakes while the material is still fresh in their minds. Luckin et al. (2016) suggest that this kind of feedback can be especially helpful for students who lack confidence to ask questions in class.

A third benefit is early support for struggling students. Learning analytics can identify warning signs, such as a student who has stopped logging in to the virtual learning environment, before the student fails an assessment. This allows the university to offer help earlier than would otherwise be possible. Finally, AI can reduce the workload of teaching staff by automating routine tasks, giving them more time to spend on the parts of teaching that require human judgement.

## Risks and ethical concerns

Despite these benefits, a number of risks and ethical concerns have been identified. The first concerns privacy and data. Personalised learning depends on collecting a large amount of data about students, and students may not always know what data is collected or how it is used. Slade and Prinsloo (2013) argue that universities have a responsibility to be transparent about their use of student data and to treat students as participants rather than simply as sources of data.

The second concern is bias. AI systems learn from past data, and if that data reflects existing inequalities, the system may reproduce them. For example, a system that predicts which students are likely to fail might unfairly label students from certain backgrounds as at risk, which could affect how they are treated.

The third concern is the role of teachers. Selwyn (2019) warns against the idea that AI can replace teachers, arguing that education is a social and relational activity and not only the transfer of information. If universities rely too heavily on AI, students may lose valuable contact with staff and with each other. There are also concerns that students could become too dependent on AI tools and not develop the ability to learn independently. Finally, the evidence that AI actually improves learning in higher education is still limited, as Zawacki-Richter et al. (2019) note that relatively few studies have involved educators or examined pedagogical outcomes in depth.

## Conclusion

This essay has described the role of AI in personalised learning in higher education. AI techniques such as adaptive learning systems, intelligent tutoring systems, learning analytics and chatbots can adapt learning to individual students. These tools offer benefits including learning at one's own pace, immediate feedback, early support for struggling students and reduced staff workload. However, there are also important risks relating to privacy, bias, the role of teachers and the limited evidence base. Overall, AI has an important role to play in personalised learning, but universities should introduce it carefully, be transparent with students about how their data is used, and ensure that AI supports rather than replaces human teaching.
